cell shows a phone's cellular service, activates it on a network, and sends and reads messages. A phone is a computer (usually a pocket computer) with a wireless modem. Once activated, it attaches to the strongest cell of its network by itself, and keeps its address and connections while moving between cells.

ex:
"cell" shows menus to read and write messages, choose a network, and more: see "cell gui" below.
"cell status" shows the network, the phone's number and address, the DNS servers and domain its network gives it (if any; see "help cellcore"), and the cells it can hear.
"cell monitor" shows the phone's service on one screen, kept up to date: its state, number and address, the cell it uses, its carrier and how strong it is, every cell it hears (of any network), the carriers it hears them on, and what has happened lately, such as moving between cells. A cell marked ">" is the one in use, and "!" one which claims to be of the phone's network but isn't; each has its carrier on the right (b20/1 is band 20's first 5MHz), and SMS after its network if it only carries messages. A cell whose site says its name (as "celld set name" gives it) has that name beside its ID, or, on a pocket computer, in its place. Anyone can give a cell any name. Under Carriers, each carrier cells were heard on has whose it is, how many cells are on it, and the strongest of them; ">" marks the one the phone uses, and grey ones were only heard a while ago. Press Q or Backspace to quit. It works before the phone is activated too, to see which networks are around, with the strongest cell in place of the one in use.
"cell locate" works out where the computer is from the cells around which say where they are (see "help celld"), as GPS does with its hosts, and how far off that may be: exact for a computer which hasn't moved lately, and one which moves works out how it's moving too, which is exact if it goes steadily in a straight line (the error allows for a turn too recent to show). Cells of the phone's own network (and partners') are used if they're enough, as their system information is signed; otherwise any. A computer without a SIM which isn't listening for cells yet listens for a few seconds first.
"cell activate ACME 7Q2M-K4XD-9PRB-T6WN-HJ3C" activates the phone on the network ACME, with a code from its administrator. Where ACME has no cells in range (or none at all), it activates through another network's cell, which passes it on to ACME if it carries ACME's phones. If two networks are called ACME, it tries each until the one which made the code answers.
"cell send 0042 Meet at the station" sends a message to the phone with the number 0042. A phone of another network which exchanges messages with this one has that network's number prefix in front: "cell send 6660042 Hello". Without service (or while cell service is off), the message waits, even if the computer restarts, and is sent once the phone has service.
"cell messages" lists the messages this phone has received and sent.
"cell networks" lists the networks around and the SIM's own: which one is in use (">"), whether the phone may use it (its "home", or a network it treats as home, a "partner" its own network has a roaming agreement with, or "unknown" when it knows of no agreement), and how strong its nearest cell is.
"cell network OTHERNET" makes the phone use the network OTHERNET only (even an unknown one, which it tries), and "cell network auto" lets it choose again: its own network where it can hear it, and otherwise a partner's (roaming). It stays on its own network until it loses it, and goes back once its own network is stronger than the one it roams on. Where it hears neither, it tries other networks too, in case they have an agreement it hasn't heard of yet; one which turns it away is left alone for a minute.
"cell bands b20 b3" makes the phone use (and listen on) those bands only ("b3", "3" and "1800" all mean band 3), and "cell bands all" any band again. "cell pin 12" pins it to cell #12 ("cell pin 12 b3/1" to that computer's cell on the carrier b3/1), as "cell status" lists them: it then uses no other cell, and has no service where it can't hear that one. "cell pin off" lets it choose again. Both are for a phone which stays where it is and chooses badly by itself, such as a relay's, which should reach the core through a cell with a good link of its own. "cell bands" and "cell pin" alone say what's set, and "cell" has both under Advanced settings.
"cell power 17" makes the phone send with 17 dBm, and "cell power default" with its default, 23 dBm: as much as a pocket computer may, so a cell (which sends with 30 dBm unless it's told otherwise, and hears 7 dB better than a pocket computer) hears the phone from as far away as the phone hears it. The power is the modem's, so everything the computer sends on it goes with that power while the phone runs; on a computer which runs a cell too, the cell's power counts. "cell power" alone says what it sends with, and what this computer may send with. "cell" has it under Advanced settings too.
"cell off" turns cell service off, and "cell on" turns it on again. While it's off, the phone doesn't listen or send, and has no address. The SIM and messages are kept.
"cell on" without a SIM makes the computer listen for cells, from when it starts too, until "cell off": the status bar says "No SIM", with the strongest cell's signal (of any network), "cell status" and "cell monitor" show the cells and networks around, and "cell locate" works at once. Like an attached phone, it listens on every carrier for five seconds each minute, and otherwise only on those it heard lately.
"cell gui", or "cell" alone, shows menus to read and write messages, to choose a network (or let the phone choose), to turn cell service off and on, and to open the monitor. Choose with Up, Down and Enter or with the mouse, go back with Q, Backspace or Left, and quit from the first menu with Q or Backspace. Messages are a conversation with each number: the list has the newest first (N writes to another number, D deletes a conversation), and a conversation has the messages from the number on the left and those sent to it on the right, with how each got on, above a line to write in. Enter sends, even without service: the message waits until there is some. There Q is just a letter: Backspace in an empty line goes back. Tab chooses a message instead, to delete it (D) or send one which wasn't sent again (R); Tab, Q or Backspace go back to writing. The top line is a status bar, in dark grey: the network's name, and the signal as four bars in brackets, such as [||..], with an R before them when roaming (R [||..]), and a note in the middle when there are new messages. There's no bar while cell service is turned off, or without a SIM, unless the computer listens for cells ("cell on").
Once the computer has a SIM (or listens for cells without one), it starts beneath the status bar: the message of the day, the startup files and the shell all run beneath it (and leaving that shell turns the computer off, as it would without the bar). "cell bar off" hides the bar, now and from when the computer starts, and "cell bar on" shows it again (the setting is cell.bar). "cell bar" runs a shell beneath the same status bar, and "cell bar <program> [arguments]" another program.
